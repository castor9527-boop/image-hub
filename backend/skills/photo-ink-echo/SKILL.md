# Photo Ink Echo

Create one vertical editorial composition from one user-provided photograph. Treat that photograph as the only content source.

Read one complete production prompt before generating:

- Chinese: references/photo-abstract-editorial-prompt.zh-CN.md
- English: references/photo-abstract-editorial-prompt.en.md

## Visual Authority

Inspect every image in assets/examples before generating. Treat those images as the highest-priority reference for hierarchy, watercolor lightness, delicate incompleteness, scale, and surrounding silence.

Use examples only as style evidence. Never copy their subjects, layouts, colors, or titles into work made from another photograph. The uploaded photograph remains the only source of content.

### Example Hierarchy Patterns

- 01-two-at-the-water: treat the seated pair and bench as one primary subject; reduce water, vegetation, and skyline to quiet traces.
- 02-still-blue-anchorage: keep the sailboat clear; use only the buoy, horizon, and reflection as pale cues.
- 03-snow-above-the-lake: keep one mountain ridge clear; reduce the tree line, lake, and foreground rocks to incomplete washes.
- 04-autumn-on-the-wall: keep one facade or window fragment clear; use red leaves and a warm wall wash as lighter cues.
- 05-white-tower-blue-sky: keep the lighthouse clear; retain only a pale blue cloud wash and the small red cap accent.
- 06-harbor-afterglow: keep one harbor or lighthouse silhouette clear; use the orange sky, mast rhythm, and reflection as softer traces.

Use these patterns to understand hierarchy and omission, not to prescribe content.

## Required Selection Logic

Do not ask:

> What is everything in this photograph?

Ask:

> What is the one thing the eye remembers first?

> What are the minimum secondary cues needed to preserve the memory of the scene?

Select exactly one primary subject. Add only two to four supporting cues. Keep the total information budget to approximately three to five visual relationships. Make everything else disappear.

The primary subject may be a person, animal, object, boat, tree, building, flower, ridge, or another single dominant visual anchor. A tightly connected group may count as one subject only when the photograph reads it as one unit.

## Workflow

1. Inspect the photograph and name one unmistakable primary subject.
2. Select two to four supporting cues that preserve essential context, such as one horizon, reflection, gesture, color wash, shadow, shoreline, mast rhythm, or nearby object relationship.
3. Reject every unselected object, background feature, texture, and repeated detail.
4. Create a 9:16 canvas with a strict horizontal 1:1 split.
5. Place the original photograph in the upper half. Allow only crop or reframe. Never repaint, stylize, reconstruct, retouch, extend, replace, or otherwise change its content.
6. Build the lower half on warm ivory or soft off-white with a barely visible fine-art watercolor-paper texture.
7. Paint the primary subject first as the clearest and most recognizable part of a very small watercolor memory motif.
8. Add the supporting cues at clearly lower contrast, lower opacity, and lower completeness. Reduce background context to faint traces, partial contours, or diluted washes.
9. Perform a deletion pass. If the motif resembles a small complete watercolor illustration, remove more objects, weaken the background, and reduce the motif.
10. Add one original poetic English title of two to five words directly below the motif in very small, thin, minimalist sans-serif type.
11. Return only the finished composition. Add no other text, labels, metadata, signatures, symbols, borders, or watermarks.

## Visual Hierarchy

- Make the primary subject immediately legible at a glance.
- Give the primary subject the strongest contour, darkest restrained accent, and greatest local contrast.
- Keep every supporting cue subordinate. Render it lighter, paler, softer, more incomplete, and less detailed than the primary subject.
- Reduce background information to the faintest possible evidence: a pale wash, disappearing edge, partial line, reflection, or small color echo.
- Never give multiple elements equal visual importance.
- Never allow supporting cues to compete with the primary subject.

## Fragmentation

Interpret fragmented as selective incompleteness, not disorder.

- Break or dissolve only parts of contours.
- Let secondary edges fade into the paper.
- Leave controlled gaps and unfinished passages.
- Keep fragments close enough to support one compact memory motif.
- Preserve a clear, recognizable primary subject.

Do not interpret fragmented as blur, random scatter, visual noise, low subject recognition, decorative splatter, or disconnected objects.

## Scale and Silence

Keep the watercolor motif very small. Use the examples as the maximum acceptable density, not as permission to enlarge or complete the scene.

Aim for a compact visual envelope of roughly 25–38% of the canvas width and roughly 8–14% of the total canvas height. Keep actual painted coverage sparse. Leave approximately 82–90% of the lower panel visually silent.

Center the motif-and-title unit near the visual center of the lower half. Do not enlarge it to occupy available space.

When in doubt, make the motif smaller and remove more information.

The final lower panel should contain:

- one clear subject
- a few quiet supporting traces
- large areas of silence around them

If it looks like a small complete watercolor illustration, it contains too much information.

If it looks like a clear but incomplete remembered fragment, it is correct.

## Watercolor Language

Use:

- light transparent watercolor
- pale diluted pigment
- delicate ink accents only where recognition requires them
- soft bleeding and disappearing edges
- incomplete color layers
- subtle dry-watercolor texture
- airy spacing
- quiet editorial restraint

Use approximately two to four source-derived color families. Keep most color low-saturation, low-contrast, and transparent. Reserve at most one darker or more saturated accent for the primary subject.

## Prohibited Results

Do not create:

- full-scene watercolor rendering
- miniature complete painting
- large watercolor motif
- too many objects
- equal visual importance between elements
- dense environment detail
- heavy rendering
- hard borders
- rectangular illustration blocks
- oval vignette
- poster-like composition
- realistic scene reconstruction
- thick paint or opaque color masses
- harsh black outlines
- decorative splatter or scattered filler
- auxiliary lines, perspective guides, grids, or annotations
- cartoon rendering

## Subject Guidance

### Architecture

Choose one building, tower, facade fragment, window, or roofline as the primary subject. Keep at most two to four faint cues such as one foliage trace, shadow, window rhythm, or color wash. Never reconstruct the full street or elevation.

### Landscape

Choose one ridge, shoreline, tree, rock, or water shape as the primary subject. Keep only the minimum horizon, reflection, foreground wash, or color trace needed for memory. Never repaint the full panorama.

### Ocean and Harbor

Choose one vessel, lighthouse, shoreline form, or reflected light as the primary subject. Keep only a few pale mast, horizon, wake, buoy, or reflection cues. Omit secondary boats unless one is essential to recognition.

### People and Animals

Choose one person, tightly connected group, or animal as the primary subject. Keep pose and identifying silhouette clear. Reduce nearby figures, setting, and objects to faint supporting traces. Do not render detailed faces.

### Plants, Food, and Small Objects

Choose one flower, plant mass, food item, or object as the primary subject. Keep only one repetition rhythm, color echo, shadow, or neighboring contour when necessary. Omit surface detail and literal texture.

## Final Self-Check

Before returning the image, verify:

1. Does the upper half contain only the original photograph, altered solely by crop or reframe?
2. Is there exactly one clear primary subject below?
3. Are there no more than two to four supporting cues?
4. Are supporting cues visibly lighter, paler, and less complete than the primary subject?
5. Has nearly all environment detail disappeared?
6. Is the motif very small and surrounded by warm-ivory silence?
7. Are fragmented edges controlled while the primary subject remains recognizable?
8. Does the lower panel avoid every prohibited full-scene, block, vignette, and poster-like treatment?
9. If the motif resembles a complete painting, has it been simplified again?
